Design an Irish Typeface · 1978

four irish typefaces

Céilí, Carrick, Kilfane and Tuam Uncial won a 1978 competition for a new Irish typeface and were sold as dry-transfer lettering, rubbed onto the page one letter at a time. Here they are again as fonts for the web and the desktop, traced letter by letter from the competition’s flyer, and now with the italics and bolds they never had.

Rub them down

In 1978 these letters came on sheets of transfer film. You laid the sheet on the page and rubbed each letter down with a pencil, placing them by eye. Pick letters off the sheet or type on the board. Hand-set adds the small wobble of letters placed by hand: on the flyer they sit up to a quarter of a millimetre off their line.

Four faces, twelve fonts

The flyer shows each face in a single style, and in 1978 that was all there was. The styles below are new: drawn in 2026 from the regulars, by rule, then checked letter by letter. Céilí and Kilfane have a bold and a bold italic, and every face has an italic. Carrick and Tuam Uncial are black already.

{{FAMILY_ROWS}}

The bolds

Each bold is its regular drawn again with a wider pen, in the face’s own contrast: Céilí’s broad flat nib spreads its strokes across, Kilfane’s rounder pen nearly as much up and down. Corners stay sharp, and the x-height, capitals and ascenders stay put, so a bold word sits level in a regular line.

A wider pen closes every narrow space at once, so the small counters (the lenses of the $, the ring of å) were drawn again with a lighter pen, and the openings that make a letter (the tails of Kilfane’s g and y) were eased open. One gap closes by choice: in the bold, the tip of Céilí’s 6 meets its stem.

The italics

Each italic is its regular narrowed by four per cent, with its stems restored, and sloped eight degrees about the middle of the x-height. A slope moves both sides of a letter alike, so the regular’s spacing and kerning carry over exactly, and accents land as they do upright. They are sloped romans, as display faces usually have: no cursive letters were invented for designers who never drew them.

No bold for Carrick or Tuam Uncial

Their stems are already more than a third of the x-height. A heavier cut would fill their counters and, in Tuam Uncial, the fine cuts that are part of Jarlath Hayes’s design. In these two, bold text keeps the one weight: the browser is told so, and never smears a fake bold over them.

The accents were on the flyer all along

Beside Kilfane’s and Carrick’s alphabets the flyer prints a small cluster of marks. Earlier versions of these fonts kept it as an ornament. It is the transfer sheet’s set of loose accents, in the face’s own style: the fada, the dot of the séimhiú, the diaeresis, ring, circumflex, háček and tilde, guillemets and two rules. Since version 1.200 every accented letter in both faces is made from them, and the dashes take their weight from the rules.

Tuam Uncial prints its own fadas on the flyer, on á é í ó ú: a long flat bar set close to the letter. The font keeps that bar and that tight setting.

Céilí has no accent set on the flyer. Its fadas are drawn in its own pen stroke, and its dots come from the flyer’s punctuation.

The competition

Letraset, Kilkenny Design Workshops and D. O’Sullivan Graphic Supplies of 66 Lower Camden Street, Dublin, sponsored a competition for a new Irish typeface. The competition brochure lists the awards: Hermann Hecht’s Céilí took first prize (£250 and a silver medallion), William Curran’s Carrick was Highly Commended (£50), and Curran’s Kilfane and Jarlath Hayes’s Tuam Uncial were each Commended (£25).

Each winner was issued on two transfer sheets, numbered S53909 to S53916. The flyer gives every sheet’s size in points and its letter height in millimetres. As far as this research found, Céilí, Carrick and Kilfane had never been digitised before.

How they were made

  1. Each letter was cut out of the flyer scan, where it stands only 20 to 35 pixels tall. Letters that touched were separated along measured cut lines.
  2. The edge of each letter was traced at sub-pixel precision and redrawn the way a type designer redraws from a scan: stems and bars as true straight lines, curves with as few points as possible, corners and serifs kept sharp.
  3. Every one of the {{N_FLYER}} glyphs printed on the flyer was then checked against the scan, one at a time. After registration the outlines follow the print to within about a quarter of a pixel, a mean overlap of {{MEAN_MATCH}}. The character inspector shows each comparison.
  4. That check found what earlier versions had missed: the sheet’s own accent set, Tuam Uncial’s tight fadas, and a Céilí h whose right leg printed starved. Its stem and wider shoulder are now the flyer’s own.
  5. Everything not on the flyer, from æ, ð, þ and ß to ⁊ and the symbols, was drawn from each face’s own parts. The fonts were spaced, kerned, given mark positioning, and checked with Font Bakery and the OpenType Sanitizer that browsers use.
  6. In 2026 each face became a family: an italic for all four, and a bold and bold italic for Céilí and Kilfane, drawn from the regulars (see Styles). Every bold was then compared with its regular, letter by letter, for counters lost or strokes run together.

How close to the flyer

{{MATCH_ROWS}}
FaceGlyphs on the flyerMean overlap

Overlap is intersection over union of the filled letter and the print, after the letter is registered on the scan. 1.000 would be identical. The rest is the scan’s own blur and the ink spread of a photocopy at 97 dpi.

A sharper source would help

A 600 to 1200 dpi rescan of the flyer, or scans of the transfer sheets or Curran’s drawings at the National Irish Visual Arts Library, would sharpen every outline. The tracing and build scripts in the full package can be rerun on a new scan.

Use them on a website

Link the stylesheet from this site and use the family names. The fonts are WOFF2 with a WOFF fallback, sent with long-lived caching and open cross-origin headers, so any site may link them directly.

HTML and CSS
<link rel="stylesheet" href="https://irish-typefaces.apps.drayne.xyz/css/four-irish-typefaces.css">

h1    { font-family: "Céilí", "Ceili", serif; }
.sign { font-family: "Carrick", serif; }
.note { font-family: "Kilfane", serif;
        font-feature-settings: "ss01"; }  /* roman forms */

<p class="note">Tír gan teanga, <b>tír gan anam</b>.</p>
/* <b>, <i>, font-weight: 700 and font-style: italic
   pick the drawn bolds and italics */

The stylesheet declares all twelve fonts, and a browser fetches only the styles a page uses. Or download the fonts and host them yourself: the ZIP has the same stylesheet with relative paths.
